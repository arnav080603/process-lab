Reactor Design: CSTR vs. PFR

Liquid-phase reaction A → B in two ideal reactors. Change the temperature, size and flow to see how conversion responds, and why the shape of a reactor matters as much as its size.

🧠 In plain English

Chemical plants make things by letting ingredients react inside big containers, a bit like baking. Here, ingredient A (blue) turns into product B (orange). Every little ✦ spark is one molecule reacting.

There are two kinds of container: a stirred tank (like a giant blender: stuff flows in, gets mixed, and flows out) and a tube reactor (a long pipe where stuff reacts as it travels, like a conveyor belt).

What to watch: the "% converted" numbers, meaning how much blue became orange. Hotter = faster reaction. Bigger reactor or slower flow = more time to react. Surprise: the tube usually beats the blender.

The engineering (for the curious)

The rate constant follows the Arrhenius equation, so temperature has an exponential effect: \(k = k_{350}\,\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{E_a}{R}\left(\tfrac{1}{T}-\tfrac{1}{350\,\text{K}}\right)\right]\).

Residence time \(\tau = V/v_0\). Mole balances for first order give \(X_{\text{CSTR}} = \dfrac{k\tau}{1+k\tau}\) and \(X_{\text{PFR}} = 1-e^{-k\tau}\). For second order: \(k C_{A0}\tau(1-X)^2 = X\) (CSTR) and \(X = \dfrac{kC_{A0}\tau}{1+kC_{A0}\tau}\) (PFR).

Try this

  • Push the target to 99%. Why does the CSTR's required volume explode?
  • Raise the temperature by 10 K. How much faster is the reaction? Compare a high and a low activation energy.
  • Switch to second order. Does the PFR's advantage grow or shrink?

Distillation: McCabe–Thiele Method

Design a column that separates a two-component mixture. Each step of the staircase is one theoretical stage, a tray where vapor and liquid reach equilibrium.

🧠 In plain English

Distillation separates two liquids that are mixed together, like getting alcohol out of water, or gasoline out of crude oil. You boil the mix at the bottom 🔥. The liquid that boils more easily rises as vapor (the bubbles) and gets cooled back into liquid at the top ❄.

Each tray inside the tower separates the mix a little more, like rinsing something again and again. Each step on the staircase graph = one tray.

What to watch: "Trays needed". You need a purer product → more trays (a taller, pricier tower). Sending more product back down the tower ("reflux") → fewer trays but a bigger energy bill. Engineers balance the two.

The engineering (for the curious)

With constant relative volatility \(\alpha\), equilibrium is \(y = \dfrac{\alpha x}{1+(\alpha-1)x}\). Above the feed, the rectifying line is \(y = \dfrac{R}{R+1}x + \dfrac{x_D}{R+1}\). The feed condition \(q\) sets the q-line \(y = \dfrac{q}{q-1}x - \dfrac{x_F}{q-1}\).

The minimum reflux \(R_{min}\) is where the operating line touches the equilibrium curve (a "pinch", which would need infinitely many stages). Fenske gives the minimum stages at total reflux: \(N_{min} = \dfrac{\ln\!\left[\frac{x_D}{1-x_D}\cdot\frac{1-x_B}{x_B}\right]}{\ln\alpha}\).

Try this

  • Slide the reflux toward 1.05 × Rmin. Watch the staircase crowd into the pinch.
  • Crank the reflux up. Fewer stages, but every unit of reflux costs reboiler energy. That's the classic design trade-off.
  • Try the hard split (α = 1.5) at 99% purity. This is why some separations need huge towers.

Process Control: PID Tank Level

A level controller (LC) reads the level transmitter (LT) and adjusts the inlet valve to hold the tank at its setpoint. The inlet pipe adds a 4-second dead time, which is what makes control hard. Tune the controller and then disturb it.

🧠 In plain English

This is cruise control for a water tank. A small computer (LC) watches the water level and opens or closes the inlet valve to keep the water at the orange dashed line (the target, or "setpoint").

The catch: water takes 4 seconds to travel down the pipe, so the computer is always acting on slightly old news, like steering a car with a delay. Real chemical plants have thousands of loops like this.

What to watch: does the blue level line reach the orange line quickly and smoothly? Click the presets: Sluggish is too gentle, Aggressive overreacts and wobbles forever, and Well tuned is just right.

The engineering (for the curious)

Tank mass balance: \(A\dfrac{dh}{dt} = F_{in}(t-\theta) - k_v\sqrt{h}\), where \(\theta\) is the pipe's dead time. The controller output is \[u = \bar u + K_c\left[e + \frac{1}{\tau_I}\int e\,dt - \tau_D\frac{dh}{dt}\right],\quad e = h_{sp}-h\]

The integral term removes offset, the derivative term acts on the measurement to avoid "derivative kick", and anti-windup stops the integral from growing while the valve is pinned fully open or shut.

Try this

  • Pick P-only and step the setpoint. The level never quite gets there: that's offset. Now add integral action.
  • Pick Aggressive. The dead time turns a big gain into growing oscillations: the loop is unstable.
  • Switch to manual, then back to automatic. The handover is "bumpless".

Heat Exchanger: Counter-current vs. Co-current

A double-pipe exchanger: hot fluid in the inner pipe, cold water in the outer shell. Size it, then flip the flow direction to see why industry almost always runs exchangers counter-current.

🧠 In plain English

Factories reuse heat instead of wasting it. Here, hot liquid in the middle pipe warms cold water flowing around it, like a hot drink warming your hands through the mug. The yellow 〰 squiggles are heat moving across.

Counter-current = the two flow in opposite directions, so the cold water keeps meeting hotter and hotter liquid as it goes. Co-current = same direction.

What to watch: "Heat moved". Flip between the two buttons and see how much more heat counter-current moves with the exact same equipment. That's why almost every real exchanger uses it.

The engineering (for the curious)

Heat duty \(Q = U A\,\Delta T_{lm}\) with the log-mean temperature difference \(\Delta T_{lm} = \dfrac{\Delta T_1-\Delta T_2}{\ln(\Delta T_1/\Delta T_2)}\). For rating an existing exchanger, the ε-NTU method avoids trial and error: \(NTU = \dfrac{UA}{C_{min}}\), and for counter-current \[\varepsilon = \frac{1-e^{-NTU(1-C_r)}}{1-C_r\,e^{-NTU(1-C_r)}},\qquad Q = \varepsilon\,C_{min}\,(T_{h,in}-T_{c,in})\]

Try this

  • Make the area huge in co-current mode. The outlets meet in the middle and can never cross.
  • Do the same in counter-current mode. The cold outlet can end up hotter than the hot outlet (a "temperature cross").
  • Compare how much area each arrangement needs for the same duty.

Lab Notebook: Measure an Activation Energy

A real kitchen experiment: time how long an effervescent tablet takes to dissolve in water at different temperatures. A faster dissolve means a bigger rate, \(k \propto 1/t\). Fit an Arrhenius plot to find the activation energy, then use it to predict.

🧠 In plain English

Warmer water makes reactions go faster. That's why sugar dissolves faster in hot tea. You can measure exactly how much faster with a fizzy tablet (like Alka-Seltzer) and a timer.

Type in how long the tablet took at each temperature. The app works out the activation energy: one number that describes how much the reaction "cares" about temperature. Bigger number = temperature matters more.

Try it: press ▶ to drop a virtual tablet, then slide the temperature and drop another.

How to run it safely

  1. Use the same volume of water (e.g. 200 mL) and the same tablet brand every time.
  2. Mix tap and kettle water to hit each temperature. Check it with a kitchen thermometer.
  3. Drop the tablet in, start a timer, and stop it when the fizzing ends.
  4. Do 5+ temperatures and repeat each one. Average the repeats.
  5. Be careful with hot water, and ask an adult before using the kettle.

The science (for the curious)

Taking the log of the Arrhenius equation turns a curve into a straight line: \(\ln k = \ln A - \dfrac{E_a}{R}\cdot\dfrac{1}{T}\). So plotting \(\ln k\) against \(1/T\) (in kelvin) gives a slope of \(-E_a/R\). Least-squares regression finds the best-fit line, and \(R^2\) tells you how well your data follows Arrhenius behavior.
